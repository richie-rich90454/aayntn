## At a glance

Berlekamp-Massey takes a finite prefix $s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_{n-1}$ of a sequence over a prime field and returns the shortest linear recurrence the prefix satisfies. The answer is an order $d$ and coefficients $c_1, \ldots, c_d$ such that

$$
s_i = c_1 s_{i-1} + c_2 s_{i-2} + \cdots + c_d s_{i-d}
$$

for every $i$ with $d \le i < n$, and no recurrence of order below $d$ fits the same prefix.

The algorithm reads the prefix once, from left to right. It keeps a connection polynomial $C(x)$, which is the current shortest recurrence, and a second polynomial $B(x)$, which remembers the correction that last forced the order to grow. At each new term it measures the discrepancy, the amount by which $C(x)$ mispredicts that term. A zero discrepancy changes nothing. A nonzero discrepancy is repaired by subtracting a shifted multiple of $B(x)$, and the order grows only when the failure proves that no shorter recurrence can fit any longer.

The cost is $O(n^2)$ time and $O(n)$ space. The naive alternative, solving a linear system for every candidate order, costs $O(n^4)$ with Gaussian elimination. The quadratic bound is the reason the technique is used: when a dynamic program can produce $2d$ terms of a sequence whose recurrence has order $d$, the recurrence is recovered in $O(d^2)$, which is the same order as writing down the $d \times d$ transition matrix once.

The algorithm needs a field. In this article the field is $\mathbb{F}_p$ for a prime $p$, and the reference programs use $p = 10^9 + 7$. Every nonzero discrepancy then has an inverse.

## Problem motivation

A token moves on a line of $k$ squares. It starts on square $1$, and each move advances it by one or two squares. The question is the number of walks of exactly $t$ moves that end on square $k$.

The count is the $(1, k)$ entry of $A^t$, where $A$ is the $k \times k$ transfer matrix with $A_{i,i+1} = A_{i,i+2} = 1$. Cayley-Hamilton says that every entry of $A^t$ satisfies a linear recurrence whose order is at most $k$, the number of states. So the answer sequence has order at most $k$, and $2k$ consecutive terms determine it in the generic case.

Generating the first $2k$ terms costs $O(k^2)$, because each term is a small dynamic program. Recovering the recurrence costs $O(k^2)$ with Berlekamp-Massey. Jumping to the $t$-th term with the recurrence costs $O(k^2 \log t)$, against $O(k^3 \log t)$ for multiplying $k \times k$ matrices. The recurrence is a compression of the process: $k$ coefficients replace $k^2$ entries of the matrix, and no matrix has to be built at all.

The second motivation is the inverse problem. A sequence is handed to the solver, and the task is to decide whether it is linear-recurrent and of what order. This is the original setting of the algorithm in coding theory, where it finds the shortest linear feedback shift register that produces a syndrome sequence in the decoding of BCH codes. The same question appears in the analysis of pseudo-random generators and in contest problems that ask for the shortest recurrence directly.

One warning belongs here. Any $n$ terms can be fit by a polynomial of degree $n-1$, so "extrapolate the sequence" is meaningless without a model. The shortest linear recurrence is a model with a small number of parameters, and Berlekamp-Massey finds the smallest one. If the data is not generated by such a model, the answer is a recurrence that fits the prefix and may predict nothing about the future. The technique is only as good as the assumption.

## Recognition patterns

Berlekamp-Massey is the right tool when the statement contains some of these phrases.

- "Find the shortest linear recurrence", "the minimal linear recurrence", "linear complexity".
- "The sequence is generated by a linear recurrence of order at most $k$".
- "Compute the $t$-th term modulo $p$" with $t$ up to $10^9$ or $10^{18}$, and a small prime modulus.
- "The first terms can be computed by dynamic programming, but $t$ is too large for the DP".
- "Count the walks", "count the tilings", "count the strings accepted by a finite automaton", where the answer is a function of the length.
- "Berlekamp-Massey", "LFSR", "linear feedback shift register", "connection polynomial".

The constraint values that rule the technique in and out:

| Situation | What it rules |
| --- | --- |
| One short sequence and only its next term | Brute force is fine; the recurrence is optional |
| Terms generated by a fixed-size DP and $t$ is huge | The intended route: recover the recurrence, then jump |
| The modulus is composite | Division can fail; use Reeds-Sloane or a field in which the inverses exist |
| The recurrence is nonlinear, with products, minima, or maxima | Nothing linear applies |
| The coefficients are given and only the $t$-th term is wanted | Kitamasa or matrix exponentiation; Berlekamp-Massey is unnecessary |
| The sequence has a closed form | Use the formula; the recurrence is a detour |
| Fewer than $2d$ terms are available | The recurrence may not be unique, and the next term may be undetermined |
| The terms are noisy or corrupted | The shortest recurrence is meaningless; use an error-correcting variant |

The penultimate row is the one that most often ruins a solution. With $n$ terms, the shortest recurrence can have order as large as $n$: the prefix of $n-1$ zeros followed by a one has no shorter recurrence, because every lower order predicts the final one as zero. A prefix shorter than twice the true order can also be fit by several recurrences. The next term computed from one of them is a guess, not a consequence. Contest problems that expect an extension either supply at least $2d$ terms or state that the shortest recurrence is unique.

## Naive approach

The baseline tries every order. For a fixed $d$, the recurrence gives one linear equation for each position $i$ with $d \le i < n$:

$$
c_1 s_{i-1} + c_2 s_{i-2} + \cdots + c_d s_{i-d} = s_i .
$$

The system has $n-d$ equations and $d$ unknowns. Solve it by Gaussian elimination, and if it is consistent, verify the candidate on every position.

```cpp
for (int d = 1; d <= n; ++d) {
    // Build the (n - d) by d system and solve it by Gaussian elimination.
    if (consistent) {
        // The candidate is the answer, because d is tried in increasing order.
        break;
    }
}
```

This is correct, and it is the reference implementation for a differential test on small inputs. Its cost is $O(n^4)$ when each order is solved from scratch, because there are $n$ orders and each solve costs $O(n^3)$ for a dense system with $n$ equations. The memory is $O(n^2)$ for the matrix.

An incremental Gaussian elimination reuses the elimination across the orders and brings the time down to $O(n^3)$, still with $O(n^2)$ memory. That is the baseline to beat, and at $n = 10^4$ it is far out of reach.

A third baseline extrapolates with the first $n$ terms as a polynomial. It always succeeds, because a degree $n-1$ polynomial passes through any $n$ points, and its next value is arbitrary. The comparison makes the point of the technique: the useful question is not "which curve passes through the data" but "which curve with the fewest parameters does".

## Key insight

The insight is that the shortest recurrence can be maintained incrementally, and a wrong prediction is repaired by adding a shifted copy of the polynomial that last forced the order to grow.

Write the candidate recurrence as a connection polynomial

$$
C(x) = 1 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + \cdots + c_L x^L .
$$

The coefficient of $s_i$ is fixed to one, so the recurrence reads $s_i + c_1 s_{i-1} + \cdots + c_L s_{i-L} = 0$. The discrepancy at position $i$ is the amount by which the recurrence fails there:

$$
d_i = s_i + c_1 s_{i-1} + \cdots + c_L s_{i-L},
$$

with terms of negative index treated as zero. If $d_i = 0$ for every $i$ with $L \le i < n$, then $C$ generates the prefix. If $d_i \ne 0$, the candidate is wrong at position $i$ and has to be corrected.

A correction has to fix position $i$ without disturbing the positions that are already correct. After the first order increase, the polynomial $B(x)$ supplies exactly such a correction. It is the polynomial saved at the last increase, it generates the prefix up to the position $r$ where it was saved, and its discrepancy at $r$ is $b \ne 0$. Shift it by $m = i - r$. Then $x^m B(x)$ has zero discrepancy at every position below $i$ and discrepancy $b$ at $i$, because the shift moves its first failure from $r$ to $i$. The combination

$$
C'(x) = C(x) - \frac{d_i}{b} x^m B(x)
$$

therefore has discrepancy $d_i - (d_i/b) b = 0$ at $i$ and is unchanged at every earlier position. It is the same correction at every step, with the division by $b$ making the amount exact. Before the first increase, $B$ is the constant polynomial $1$ and the correction raises the order to $i+1$ instead; position $i$ then becomes an initial condition rather than a constraint.

The second insight is the order update. If $2L \le i$, the failure at $i$ proves that no polynomial of degree below $i+1-L$ can generate the longer prefix, so the order has to grow to exactly $i+1-L$. If $2L > i$, the order $L$ is still minimal and only the coefficients change. The condition is not a heuristic; it is the exact point at which the old order becomes impossible.

## Visual intuition

Take the sequence

$$
0,\ 1,\ 1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 11,\ 21, \ldots
$$

It satisfies $s_i = s_{i-1} + 2 s_{i-2}$, so the final connection polynomial is $C(x) = 1 - x - 2x^2$. Watch the polynomial change as the prefix grows. The coefficients are shown as signed integers; in the programs they are residues modulo $10^9+7$.

| Prefix | Shortest order | Connection polynomial | Why it changed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $1$ | The empty recurrence already generates the zero prefix |
| $0, 1$ | $2$ | $1 - x^2$ | Order $1$ would force $s_1 = c_1 s_0 = 0$ |
| $0, 1, 1$ | $2$ | $1 - x - x^2$ | The first coefficient becomes $1$ |
| $0, 1, 1, 3$ | $2$ | $1 - x - 2x^2$ | The second coefficient becomes $2$ |
| $0, 1, 1, 3, 5$ | $2$ | $1 - x - 2x^2$ | The new term agrees |
| $0, 1, 1, 3, 5, 11$ | $2$ | $1 - x - 2x^2$ | The new term agrees |
| $0, 1, 1, 3, 5, 11, 21$ | $2$ | $1 - x - 2x^2$ | The recurrence is confirmed |

The second row is worth pausing on. After two terms the shortest recurrence already has order $2$, and it stays $2$ for the rest of the run; what changes is the coefficients. With only $s_0 = 0$ and $s_1 = 1$, an order-$1$ recurrence would have to satisfy $1 = c_1 \cdot 0$, which is impossible, so the algorithm has to allow two coefficients. The next term then fixes the first coefficient, and the term after that fixes the second.

Picture the two polynomials as windows on the sequence. $C$ is a window of width $L+1$ that starts at the current position and slides one step per iteration. $B$ is an older window, frozen at the position where the order last grew. When the current window mispredicts, the algorithm subtracts a shifted copy of the frozen window, weighted by the ratio of the two discrepancies. The subtraction changes the current window only from the point where the shifted copy starts, which is the current position; everything before that is already correct and stays untouched.

The length update is the other half of the picture. A window of width $L+1$ can be correct at $i$ and still be too narrow to be correct at $i+1$; the failure is the proof that it is too narrow. When $2L \le i$, the algorithm widens the window to $i+1-L$. When $2L > i$, the window is wide enough and only its coefficients move.

## Formal model

Let $p$ be a prime and let the sequence be $s_0, s_1, \ldots$ with $s_i \in \mathbb{F}_p$. Only a finite prefix of length $n$ is given.

::: math definition Definition
A connection polynomial of order $L$ is a polynomial

$$
C(x) = 1 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + \cdots + c_L x^L
$$

with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}_p$. Its discrepancy at position $i$ is

$$
d_i(C) = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(L,i)} C_j s_{i-j},
$$

where $C_0 = 1$. The polynomial generates the prefix $s_0, \ldots, s_{N-1}$ if $d_i(C) = 0$ for every $i$ with $L \le i < N$.
:::

::: math definition Definition
The minimal order of a prefix is the smallest $L$ for which some connection polynomial of order $L$ generates it. The sequence has linear complexity $L$ over the prefix when the minimal order is $L$.
:::

The empty recurrence is the polynomial $C(x) = 1$ of order $0$. Its only discrepancy is $d_i = s_i$, so it generates a prefix exactly when every term of the prefix is zero.

::: math lemma Lemma
A connection polynomial $C$ of order $L$ generates $s_0, \ldots, s_{N-1}$ if and only if the polynomial $(C(x) S(x)) \bmod x^N$ has degree less than $L$, where $S(x) = \sum_{i \ge 0} s_i x^i$.
:::

::: math proof Proof
The coefficient of $x^i$ in $C(x) S(x)$ is $\sum_{j=0}^{\min(L,i)} C_j s_{i-j} = d_i(C)$. If $C$ generates the prefix, those coefficients vanish for $L \le i < N$, so the truncated product has no terms of degree $L$ or above. Conversely, if the truncated product has degree below $L$, its coefficients at degrees $L$ through $N-1$ are zero, which is exactly the generating condition. $\square$
:::

::: math lemma Lemma
Let $C$ be a connection polynomial of order $L$ that generates the prefix of length $i$, and let $d = d_i(C) \ne 0$. Let $B$ be a connection polynomial of order $L_B$ that generates the prefix of length $r < i$, let $b = d_r(B) \ne 0$, and let $m = i - r$. Define

$$
C'(x) = C(x) - \frac{d}{b} x^m B(x).
$$

Then $C'$ generates the prefix of length $i+1$.
:::

::: math proof Proof
Let $L' = \max(L, m + L_B)$. The polynomial $C'$ has order at most $L'$, so only positions $L' \le j < i+1$ have to be checked. If $L' = i+1$, that range is empty and the claim is immediate. Otherwise $m + L_B \le i$, so $r = i - m \ge 0$ and the backup is a genuine prefix generator.

For $L' \le j < i$, the discrepancy of $C$ is zero because $j \ge L$, and the discrepancy of $x^m B$ is zero because $j - m \le i - 1 - m = r - 1$ and $j - m \ge L_B$. Both terms vanish. At $j = i$, the discrepancy of $C$ is $d$ and the discrepancy of $x^m B$ is $b$, so the discrepancy of $C'$ is $d - (d/b) b = 0$. Therefore $C'$ generates the prefix of length $i+1$. $\square$
:::

::: math lemma Lemma
Let $C$ be a connection polynomial of order $L$ that is minimal for the prefix of length $i$, and let $d = d_i(C) \ne 0$. Any connection polynomial $D$ of order $K$ that generates the prefix of length $i+1$ satisfies $K \ge i+1-L$ whenever $2L \le i$.
:::

::: math proof Proof
Write $S(x)$ for the formal series of the sequence. Because $C$ generates the prefix of length $i$ and fails at $i$, there is a polynomial $P$ of degree below $L$ with

$$
C(x) S(x) \equiv P(x) + d x^i \pmod{x^{i+1}} .
$$

Because $D$ generates the prefix of length $i+1$, there is a polynomial $Q$ of degree below $K$ with

$$
D(x) S(x) \equiv Q(x) \pmod{x^{i+1}} .
$$

Multiply the first congruence by $D(x)$ and the second by $C(x)$:

$$
C(x) Q(x) \equiv D(x) P(x) + d D(x) x^i \pmod{x^{i+1}} .
$$

Suppose $K < i+1-L$. Then $L + K < i+1$, so both $CQ$ and $DP$ have degree below $i$, and the coefficient of $x^i$ on the left side is zero. On the right side, the same coefficient is $d \cdot D_0 = d \ne 0$, a contradiction. Hence $K \ge i+1-L$. $\square$
:::

::: math theorem Theorem
After processing the prefix of length $n$, Berlekamp-Massey returns a connection polynomial of minimal order for that prefix.
:::

::: math proof Proof
The proof is an induction on the position $i$. At the start, $C(x) = 1$ has order $0$, which is minimal for the empty prefix.

Assume $C$ is minimal for the prefix of length $i$, with order $L$. If $d_i(C) = 0$, the same polynomial generates the longer prefix. Any polynomial that generates the longer prefix also generates the shorter one, so its order is at least $L$, and $L$ stays minimal.

If $d_i(C) \ne 0$, the correction lemma produces a polynomial $C'$ that generates the prefix of length $i+1$. Its order is at most $\max(L, m + L_B)$. Let $r$ be the position at which $B$ was saved. Then $L = r + 1 - L_B$, so $m + L_B = i - r + r + 1 - L = i + 1 - L$.

If $2L \le i$, this bound is $i+1-L$, and the lower-bound lemma says that no polynomial of smaller order generates the longer prefix. The order is raised to exactly $i+1-L$.

If $2L > i$, the bound $i+1-L$ is at most $L$. The corrected polynomial has order at most $L$, and no polynomial of smaller order can generate the longer prefix, because such a polynomial would also generate the shorter one and would contradict the minimality of $L$. So the order stays $L$, and the polynomial is updated.

In both cases the new order is minimal for the prefix of length $i+1$, which completes the induction. For the initial backup $B = 1$, read the save position as $r = -1$; then $L = r + 1 - L_B$ is $0 = 0$, and the same algebra applies. $\square$
:::

The theorem covers the case $L = 0$ as well. The all-zero prefix never produces a discrepancy, the order stays $0$, and the polynomial stays $1$. Any other prefix produces a nonzero discrepancy at its first nonzero term, and the order becomes $1$ at that point.

## Algorithm derivation

The state consists of four objects.

- $C$, the current connection polynomial, stored as the coefficients $C_0 = 1, C_1, \ldots, C_L$.
- $B$, the last polynomial that forced an order increase.
- $b$, the discrepancy of $B$ at the position $r$ where it was saved.
- $m = i - r$, the number of positions since that save.

The loop is:

1. Compute the discrepancy $d$ of $C$ at position $i$.
2. If $d = 0$, increment $m$ and continue.
3. Otherwise copy $C$ into $T$, and compute $\mathit{coef} = d / b$.
4. Extend $C$ if necessary, then subtract $\mathit{coef} \cdot x^m B$ from $C$.
5. If $2L \le i$, set $L = i + 1 - L$, replace $B$ with $T$, set $b = d$, and reset $m = 1$.
6. Otherwise increment $m$.

```cpp
std::vector<long long> C(1, 1), B(1, 1);
int L = 0, m = 1;
long long b = 1;

for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
    long long d = 0;
    for (int j = 0; j <= L && j <= i; ++j) {
        d = (d + C[j] * s[i - j]) % MOD;
    }
    if (d == 0) {
        ++m;
        continue;
    }
    std::vector<long long> T = C;
    long long coef = d * power(b, MOD - 2) % MOD;
    if (C.size() < B.size() + m) C.resize(B.size() + m, 0);
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < B.size(); ++j) {
        C[j + m] = (C[j + m] - coef * B[j] % MOD + MOD) % MOD;
    }
    if (2 * L <= i) {
        L = i + 1 - L;
        B = T;
        b = d;
        m = 1;
    } else {
        ++m;
    }
}
```

The copy $T$ must be taken before $C$ is modified, because $B$ has to be the polynomial that was in force when the order grew, not the corrected one. The multiplication by $x^m$ is just an index shift, so the correction writes $C[j+m]$ and leaves the first $m$ coefficients alone.

After the loop, the recurrence is $s_i = -C_1 s_{i-1} - \cdots - C_L s_{i-L}$, and the next term is

$$
s_n = -\sum_{j=1}^{L} C_j s_{n-j} \bmod p,
$$

with the empty sum equal to zero when $L = 0$. No extension of the recurrence is needed for one extra term; the formula is the recurrence applied at position $n$.

## Step-by-step execution

Use the sequence $0, 1, 1, 3, 5, 11, 21$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ with a large prime $p$. Coefficients are written as signed integers; a negative coefficient such as $-1$ stands for $p-1$ in the programs.

Start with $L = 0$, $C = [1]$, $B = [1]$, $b = 1$, $m = 1$.

| $i$ | $s_i$ | Discrepancy $d$ | Shift | $L$ after | $C$ after     | $m$ after |
| --- | ----- | --------------- | ----- | --------- | ------------- | --------- |
| 0   | 0     | 0               | none  | 0         | $[1]$         | 2         |
| 1   | 1     | 1               | 2     | 2         | $[1, 0, -1]$  | 1         |
| 2   | 1     | 1               | 1     | 2         | $[1, -1, -1]$ | 2         |
| 3   | 3     | 1               | 2     | 2         | $[1, -1, -2]$ | 3         |
| 4   | 5     | 0               | none  | 2         | $[1, -1, -2]$ | 4         |
| 5   | 11    | 0               | none  | 2         | $[1, -1, -2]$ | 5         |
| 6   | 21    | 0               | none  | 2         | $[1, -1, -2]$ | 6         |

The first row ends with $m = 2$ because the zero discrepancy of $s_0$ did not change the order but did advance the distance from the last save.

At $i = 1$, the discrepancy is $1$, the shift is $m = 2$, and $C$ becomes $[1] - x^2 \cdot [1] = [1, 0, -1]$. The condition $2L \le i$ is $0 \le 1$, so the order becomes $1 + 1 - 0 = 2$. The old polynomial $[1]$ is saved as $B$, and $m$ resets to $1$.

At $i = 2$, the discrepancy is $1 + 0 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 0 = 1$. The shift is now $1$, so $C$ becomes $[1, 0, -1] - x \cdot [1] = [1, -1, -1]$. The condition $4 \le 2$ is false, so the order stays $2$ and $m$ becomes $2$.

At $i = 3$, the discrepancy is $3 - 1 - 1 = 1$. The shift is $2$, so $C$ becomes $[1, -1, -1] - x^2 \cdot [1] = [1, -1, -2]$. The order stays $2$ and $m$ becomes $3$.

The last three terms agree with $C(x) = 1 - x - 2x^2$, so their discrepancies are zero. The final recurrence is $s_i = s_{i-1} + 2s_{i-2}$, and the next term is $21 + 2 \cdot 11 = 43$.

The trace also shows why the shift is a variable. If the correction at $i = 1$ had used shift $1$, it would have written at index $1$ and produced $[1, -1]$ with a claimed order of $2$; at $i = 2$ the algorithm would then be correcting the wrong coefficient, and the final recurrence would not be the one in the last row.

## Correctness proof

The correctness result is the theorem of the formal model. This section restates its parts in the order in which they are used.

The generating-function lemma turns a statement about discrepancies into a statement about one truncated product. It is the bridge between the loop and the algebra: a polynomial generates a prefix exactly when its product with the sequence has no high coefficients.

The correction lemma is local. It says that one shifted copy of $B$ cancels the failure at the current position and touches nothing that is already correct. The proof has three index ranges. Positions below the new order are not required to satisfy anything. Positions from the new order up to $i-1$ see a zero discrepancy from $C$, because the new order is at least the old one, and a zero discrepancy from the shifted $B$, because the shift puts the failure of $B$ at $i$ and every earlier position of $B$ is already correct. Position $i$ sees $d$ from $C$ and $b$ from the shifted $B$, and the ratio $d/b$ cancels them.

The lower-bound lemma is the reason the order update is forced. It compares the two generating polynomials $C$ and $D$ through their products with the sequence. If a polynomial of order $K$ generated one more term while $L + K < i+1$, the products $CQ$ and $DP$ would both have degree below $i$, and the coefficient of $x^i$ would be zero on one side of an identity and $d \ne 0$ on the other. That contradiction is exactly the statement that $K$ is too small.

The induction then puts the pieces together. A zero discrepancy keeps the minimal order. A nonzero discrepancy with $2L \le i$ raises the order to the lower bound, and the corrected polynomial meets it. A nonzero discrepancy with $2L > i$ keeps the order and changes only the coefficients, and the new polynomial still generates the longer prefix. After $n$ steps the returned polynomial is minimal for the whole prefix.

The theorem says nothing about the true sequence beyond the prefix. The returned order is the smallest that fits the data, not necessarily the order of the process that produced the data. That distinction is the subject of the recognition section and of the practice progression.

## Complexity analysis

| Method                                           | Time                  | Space    |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------- | -------- |
| Solve one dense system per order                 | $O(n^4)$              | $O(n^2)$ |
| Incremental Gaussian elimination                 | $O(n^3)$              | $O(n^2)$ |
| Berlekamp-Massey                                 | $O(n^2)$              | $O(n)$   |
| Berlekamp-Massey and one next term               | $O(n^2)$              | $O(n)$   |
| Berlekamp-Massey and the $t$-th term by Kitamasa | $O(n^2 + L^2 \log t)$ | $O(n)$   |
| Matrix exponentiation of a $d$-state process     | $O(d^3 \log t)$       | $O(d^2)$ |

Each iteration computes one discrepancy, a dot product of at most $L+1$ terms, and at most one correction, a loop over at most $L_B+1$ terms. Both lengths are at most $n$, so one iteration costs $O(n)$ and the whole loop costs $O(n^2)$.

The modular inverse costs $O(\log p)$ by fast exponentiation and is computed only when the discrepancy is nonzero, so it adds $O(n \log p)$ in the worst case, which is dominated by $O(n^2)$ for $n$ larger than $\log p$.

The space is three arrays of at most $n+1$ coefficients plus the input sequence. The arrays are the current polynomial, the saved polynomial, and the copy taken before each correction. The copy can be avoided with a more careful in-place scheme, but the bound is already $O(n)$.

At $n = 500$, the loop performs about $10^5$ coefficient operations and the inverse dominates nothing. At $n = 10^4$, it performs about $10^8$, which is the practical limit for a compiled language and is slow in Python. The quadratic bound is the intended one for the technique; faster algorithms exist through polynomial multiplication and the half-gcd, and they are mentioned in the variants section.

The number of terms needed is the other half of the cost. If the process has $d$ states, $2d$ terms suffice in the generic case, so the total cost of recovering the recurrence is $O(d^2)$ and the cost of the far term is $O(d^2 \log t)$. Both are far below the $O(d^3 \log t)$ of a dense matrix, and neither requires the transition matrix to be written down.

## Algorithm selection

| Situation | What to use |
| --- | --- |
| The shortest recurrence of a given prefix | Berlekamp-Massey |
| Only the next term, recurrence unknown | Berlekamp-Massey, then one evaluation |
| The $t$-th term, recurrence known | Kitamasa or matrix exponentiation |
| The $t$-th term, recurrence unknown, prefix cheap | Berlekamp-Massey and Kitamasa |
| The order is close to the number of terms | Berlekamp-Massey; the system methods are worse |
| The modulus is composite | Reeds-Sloane, or work in a field extension |
| The recurrence is nonlinear | No linear method applies |
| The sequence has a rational generating function | Berlekamp-Massey, or the Euclidean algorithm on the numerator and denominator |
| The terms are noisy | Reeds-Sloane or Berlekamp-Welch, not plain Berlekamp-Massey |
| The terms are known only through an oracle | Query at least $2d$ terms and verify on held-out terms |

The first row is the direct use. The fourth row is the contest use, and it has two costs: the prefix generation and the recurrence evaluation. The prefix generation is usually the expensive part, because each term costs the work of one DP layer. If the process has a small state space, the DP layer is cheap and the whole route is fast.

The composite-modulus row is a real restriction, not a formality. The correction divides by $b$, and if $b$ shares a factor with the modulus, the inverse does not exist. Reeds-Sloane extends the algorithm to prime powers, and the Chinese remainder theorem assembles a result modulo a composite from its prime-power pieces. If the problem's modulus is composite and the recurrence is needed, that is the route.

The noisy row is the coding-theory connection. When a few terms are wrong, the shortest recurrence that fits all of them is usually the one that memorizes the errors, with a large order. The error-correcting versions look for the shortest recurrence that fits all but a bounded number of terms.

## Reference implementations

The six `program` variants implement one contract:

1. Read $n$.
2. Read $n$ terms $s_0, \ldots, s_{n-1}$, each a residue modulo $10^9+7$.
3. Print the order $L$ of the shortest recurrence and the next term $s_n$, separated by one space.

The output is one line, and the order is printed even when it is zero. For the zero prefix the next term is $0$, because the empty recurrence predicts zero. The input is whitespace-separated, so a line break may appear anywhere between two numbers.

| Language | File | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | `c/program.c` | Arrays of `long long` with an explicit swap of the saved polynomial |
| C++17 | `cpp17/program.cpp` | The same loop with `std::vector<long long>` |
| Java | `java/Main.java` | The class `Main`, a byte-buffer scanner, and `long` arithmetic |
| Python | `python/program.py` | Lists and arbitrary-precision integers |
| Pascal | `pascal/program.pas` | `Int64` coefficients and an exact modular product written for pas2js |
| JavaScript | `javascript/program.js` | `BigInt` coefficients, because products exceed the safe integer range |

All six use the same discrepancy loop, the same correction, and the same order condition, so they produce byte-identical output on the shared fixtures. The arithmetic choices differ only where the language forces them.

The C and C++ variants reduce after every product and keep the coefficients in `[0, p)`. Java does the same with `long`; a product of two residues below $10^9+7$ is below $10^{18}$, which fits comfortably in a signed 64-bit integer. Python uses its arbitrary-precision integers and never overflows. JavaScript uses `BigInt`, because a product of two residues near $10^9$ is about $10^{18}$ and a `Number` loses the low bits above $2^{53}$. Pascal uses `Int64` and an exact product that splits one factor at $2^{15}$, so every intermediate product stays below $2^{53}$ even when the program is compiled by pas2js.

The JavaScript and Pascal listings are educational references. Neither language is a USACO submission language. Both sources begin with the banner. The Pascal source uses `SysUtils`, reads one argument per `Read`, and avoids `IOResult` and 128-bit integers, so it compiles under pas2js as well as under Free Pascal.

The manifest publishes only the `program` variant. There is no reusable `minimal` variant, because the technique is one loop over the input and a caller who wants to reuse it needs the same four state variables; a class would hide the discrepancy and the shift without making either clearer.

## Recursion decision

The algorithm is iterative, and it should stay iterative. The outer loop runs over the positions, the discrepancy is a loop over the coefficients, and the correction is a second loop over the coefficients. No step has a recursive structure.

A recursive formulation would recurse on the prefix: "process the first $i$ terms, then handle term $i$". The depth would be $n$, and the work at each level would be the same discrepancy computation, so the recursion would add $O(n)$ stack frames for no gain. At $n = 10^4$ that is safe in C and C++ and marginal in Python, but there is no reason to pay for it.

The only place where recursion naturally appears is the evaluation of the $t$-th term. The polynomial exponentiation that implements Kitamasa recurses to depth $O(\log t)$, which is at most about $60$ for a 64-bit index. That recursion is safe in every language and is a separate algorithm.

| Language | Practical depth | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C and C++ | About $10^5$ frames on an 8 MB stack | Iterative; recursion is unnecessary |
| Java | About $10^4$ frames | Iterative |
| Python | The interpreter limit of 1000 | Iterative; a recursive prefix would exceed it at $n > 1000$ |
| Pascal | The operating system stack | Iterative |
| JavaScript | About $10^4$ frames | Iterative |

The general rule is that a loop whose state is one polynomial should be written as a loop. The recursion that is worth considering is the divide-and-conquer product in a fast polynomial algorithm, and that is a different technique with a different complexity.

## Implementation walkthrough

Five details decide whether a Berlekamp-Massey implementation is correct.

The discrepancy uses the first $L+1$ coefficients only. The update may leave coefficients beyond the current order in the array, because the correction writes at index $j+m$ and the array can be longer than the order. Those coefficients are not part of the current recurrence, and including them in the dot product changes the discrepancy and breaks the invariant. The loop bound is `j <= L`, not the array length.

The saved polynomial is copied before the correction. When the order grows, $B$ has to be the polynomial that was in force at the failure, not the polynomial after the failure was repaired. The order of the copy and the update is therefore fixed:

```cpp
std::vector<long long> T = C;          // the old polynomial
// ... modify C ...
if (2 * L <= i) {
    B = T;                             // the old polynomial is the backup
    b = d;
    m = 1;
}
```

The shift is $m$, not one. The variable counts the positions since the last save and is reset to one at every order increase. Using one instead of $m$ writes the correction at the wrong index: the polynomial still changes, but it no longer has zero discrepancy at the positions that were already correct.

The division by $b$ is part of the correction. The amount that has to be canceled at the current position is $d$, and the shifted backup contributes $b$ there, so the multiplier is $d/b$. Forgetting the division leaves a polynomial that may fit the first few terms by accident and then predicts the wrong next term. The smallest example is the two-term sequence $2, 1$ modulo $7$: the correct recurrence is $s_i = 4s_{i-1}$ and the next term is $4$, while the version without the division produces $s_i = 6s_{i-1}$ and reports $6$.

The sign convention is easy to reverse. The connection polynomial is $1 + c_1 x + \cdots$, so the recurrence is $s_i = -c_1 s_{i-1} - \cdots$. The next term is the negative of the dot product, reduced into $[0, p)$. A program that prints the dot product directly is off by a sign, and the error is invisible when all coefficients happen to be zero.

A compact Python core that follows the same state machine is:

```python
MOD = 1_000_000_007
C, B = [1], [1]
order, shift, last = 0, 1, 1

for i in range(n):
    d = sum(C[j] * s[i - j] for j in range(min(order, i) + 1)) % MOD
    if d == 0:
        shift += 1
        continue
    saved = C[:]
    coef = d * pow(last, MOD - 2, MOD) % MOD
    if len(C) < len(B) + shift:
        C += [0] * (len(B) + shift - len(C))
    for j in range(len(B)):
        C[j + shift] = (C[j + shift] - coef * B[j]) % MOD
    if 2 * order <= i:
        order = i + 1 - order
        B = saved
        last = d
        shift = 1
    else:
        shift += 1

next_term = 0
for j in range(1, order + 1):
    next_term = (next_term + C[j] * s[n - j]) % MOD
next_term = (-next_term) % MOD
```

The core is short because the state is small. The six programs differ only in how they store the coefficients and how they multiply residues; the control flow is the same.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | How to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Updating the order on every nonzero discrepancy | The reported order is too large | Update only when $2L \le i$ |
| Saving $C$ after the correction | The backup is not the polynomial that failed, so later corrections are wrong | Copy $C$ into $T$ before modifying it |
| Using shift $1$ instead of $m$ | The correction lands at the wrong position and earlier terms stop being generated | Keep $m$ and reset it only at an order increase |
| Forgetting to divide by $b$ | The corrected polynomial may not generate the prefix, and the next term is wrong | Multiply by the modular inverse of $b$ |
| Using the dot product as the recurrence | The next term has the wrong sign | The recurrence coefficients are the negatives of $C_1, \ldots, C_L$ |
| Including coefficients beyond the order | The discrepancy is computed against the wrong polynomial | Sum only $j = 0, \ldots, L$ |
| Working modulo a composite | The inverse of $b$ may not exist | Use a prime modulus, or Reeds-Sloane |
| Extending from fewer than $2L$ terms | The next term is not determined by the data | Require enough terms, or verify on held-out terms |
| Assuming the order is at most the input size divided by two | The prefix may have a larger order | The order can reach $n$ in the worst case |
| Recomputing the inverse by trial division | The loop becomes slow or wrong | Use fast exponentiation modulo a prime |

A concrete failure is worth tracing. Take the two-term sequence $2, 1$ modulo $7$. The correct run starts with $C = [1]$, sees $d = 2$ at $i = 0$, sets $C = [1, -2]$, and saves $B = [1]$, $b = 2$. At $i = 1$ the discrepancy is $1 + (-2) \cdot 2 = -3 \equiv 4$. The shift is $1$, the inverse of $2$ is $4$, so the multiplier is $4 \cdot 4 = 2$ modulo $7$, and $C$ becomes $[1, -2 - 2] = [1, 3]$. The recurrence is $s_i = -3 s_{i-1} = 4 s_{i-1}$, which gives $s_2 = 4$ and agrees with $1 = 4 \cdot 2$ modulo $7$.

The version that forgets the inverse uses multiplier $4$ and produces $C = [1, -2 - 4] = [1, 1]$, which claims $s_i = -s_{i-1} = 6s_{i-1}$. That recurrence gives $s_2 = 6$ and does not even generate $s_1$, because $6 \cdot 2 = 12 \equiv 5 \ne 1$. A verification pass over the prefix catches the second version immediately. A program that trusts the loop and prints the next term does not.

The smallest instance in which the sign error changes the next term is the same pair: the correct next term is $4$, the negated version reports $3$. One position is not enough to expose either bug, because the first term is always consistent with the empty recurrence and no split is made.

## Variants and extensions

- **Kitamasa and the $t$-th term.** Once the recurrence is known, the $t$-th term is the coefficient of $x^t$ in a rational generating function. Reducing $x^t$ modulo the characteristic polynomial by binary exponentiation costs $O(L^2 \log t)$, and the answer is the dot product of the reduced polynomial with the first $L$ terms.
- **Bostan-Mori.** The same far-term problem solved by halving the rational function at each step. The cost is $O(L \log L \log t)$ with a fast polynomial multiplication, and it avoids the $L^2$ coefficient work when $L$ is large.
- **Fast Berlekamp-Massey.** The discrepancy and the correction are convolutions, so both can be computed with FFT or NTT. The half-gcd formulation of the same computation gives $O(n \log^2 n)$, at the price of a much larger implementation.
- **Reeds-Sloane.** The extension from a field to the ring of integers modulo a prime power. The inverse of $b$ is replaced by a careful scaling, and the result is assembled with the Chinese remainder theorem when the modulus has several prime factors.
- **Berlekamp-Welch and error correction.** When a few terms are corrupted, the shortest recurrence that fits all but a bounded number of terms is the message, and the algorithm becomes a decoder for Reed-Solomon codes.
- **The minimal polynomial of a matrix.** Cayley-Hamilton says that a matrix satisfies its characteristic polynomial, and the minimal polynomial is the shortest recurrence of the matrix powers. Berlekamp-Massey on one matrix entry recovers a divisor of the minimal polynomial, which is enough for many sequence problems.
- **Linear feedback shift registers.** The original formulation: the connection polynomial is the feedback polynomial of a shift register, and the linear complexity is the length of the shortest register that produces the stream. The pseudo-random sequences used in communication have a small linear complexity exactly when the generator is weak.
- **The generating-function view.** A sequence is linearly recurrent exactly when its generating function is rational. The denominator is the reversed connection polynomial, and the Euclidean algorithm on the numerator and denominator is the polynomial version of the same computation.

The variants split into two families. The first family changes the model: ring instead of field, errors instead of exact terms, matrix instead of scalar. The second family changes the speed: convolution instead of the quadratic inner loops, half-gcd instead of the step-by-step correction. The core loop in this article is the reference point for both.

## Contest strategy

1. Decide whether a linear recurrence is plausible. The usual evidence is a fixed-size DP whose state does not depend on $t$, or a rational generating function.
2. Make sure the modulus is prime. If it is not, check whether the inverses you need exist, or switch to Reeds-Sloane.
3. Generate more than $2d$ terms. The theoretical minimum is $2d$; a few extra terms let you verify the recovered recurrence on held-out values.
4. Run Berlekamp-Massey on the prefix. If the returned order is larger than half the number of terms, the prefix is too short to certify the extension; generate more terms before trusting it.
5. Verify the recurrence on every term of the prefix and on the held-out terms. Verification is one pass and catches sign errors, index errors, and wrong moduli.
6. Evaluate the wanted term with Kitamasa rather than with a matrix. The matrix route costs $O(L^3 \log t)$ and is the bottleneck when $L$ is in the thousands.
7. Keep the arithmetic exact. In C and Java, reduce after every product; in JavaScript, use `BigInt`; in Pascal, use an exact product that stays below $2^{53}$.
8. Print the order and the term in the required format. If the problem wants coefficients, remember the sign convention and the required number of coefficients.

The third and fifth steps are the ones that separate a working solution from a lucky one. A recurrence recovered from too few terms can fit the prefix and predict nonsense. A recurrence that is not verified can be off by a sign or an index and still look plausible on the first few terms.

## Worked problems

### Problem 1: the shortest recurrence itself

**Restatement.** This is [Find Linear Recurrence](https://judge.yosupo.jp/problem/find_linear_recurrence) on Library Checker. The input is a prefix of a sequence modulo a prime, and the output is the order and the coefficients of the shortest linear recurrence that generates the prefix.

**Naive baseline.** For each order $d$, solve the linear system for the coefficients and verify it. The cost is $O(n^4)$ with a dense solver, and the matrix is $O(n^2)$ in memory.

**Insight.** The shortest recurrence for each prefix can be maintained with one discrepancy and, when the discrepancy is nonzero, one shifted correction.

**Derivation.** Run the loop with the connection polynomial $C$, the backup $B$, the saved discrepancy $b$, and the shift $m$. At the end, the order is $L$ and the recurrence coefficients are $-C_1, \ldots, -C_L$.

**Correctness.** The induction of the correctness proof shows that the returned polynomial is minimal for the prefix, and the minimal polynomial is the requested answer.

**Complexity.** $O(n^2)$ time and $O(n)$ space.

**Common wrong approach.** Printing $C_1, \ldots, C_L$ without negating them. The polynomial $1 + c_1 x + \cdots$ represents $s_i + c_1 s_{i-1} + \cdots = 0$, so the recurrence coefficients are the negatives.

### Problem 2: the template with a far term

**Restatement.** This is [P5487, the Berlekamp-Massey template](https://www.luogu.com.cn/problem/P5487) on Luogu. The input is $n$ terms and an index $m$ with $n < m \le 10^9$ and $n \le 10^4$, and the output is the shortest recurrence modulo $998244353$ followed by the $m$-th term.

**Naive baseline.** Run Berlekamp-Massey in $O(n^2)$, then evaluate the $m$-th term with matrix exponentiation in $O(L^3 \log m)$, where $L$ can reach $5000$. That is far too slow.

**Insight.** The recurrence is known after the first phase, so the far term is a polynomial exponentiation problem rather than a matrix problem.

**Derivation.** Let the recurrence have order $L$ and characteristic polynomial $Q(x) = x^L - c_1 x^{L-1} - \cdots - c_L$. Reduce $x^m$ modulo $Q(x)$ by binary exponentiation, keeping a polynomial of degree below $L$. If $x^m \equiv a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{L-1} x^{L-1}$, then the answer is $\sum a_i s_i$.

**Correctness.** The reduction is the Cayley-Hamilton identity for the shift operator: every power of $x$ above $L$ is replaced by the recurrence, so the coefficient vector of the reduced polynomial gives exactly the linear combination of the first $L$ terms that equals the $m$-th term.

**Complexity.** $O(n^2 + L^2 \log m)$ time and $O(L)$ space.

**Common wrong approach.** Using matrix exponentiation. It is correct but the constant is too large for $L = 5000$; the polynomial route is the intended one. A second wrong approach is to forget that the far index can be smaller than the number of terms; the statement excludes it, but a reusable routine should return the given term directly in that case.

### Problem 3: the counting problem behind the recurrence

**Restatement.** This is [Mr. Kitayuta's Gift](https://codeforces.com/problemset/problem/506/E). A string $s$ of at most $200$ lowercase letters is given, together with an integer $n$ up to $10^9$. Exactly $n$ letters are inserted into $s$, and the task is to count the distinct palindromes that can be obtained, modulo $10007$.

**Naive baseline.** An interval dynamic program over the pairs of positions that are matched from the two ends. For small $n$ it computes the answer directly, but $n$ is too large for a layer-by-layer DP.

**Insight.** The interval DP can be reduced to a graph with $O(|s|)$ boundary states: the two ends of the unmatched part of $s$, together with a flag for whether the middle has been reached. Each inserted letter either matches the current pair of characters or advances one side, so the transition is linear and does not depend on the index. Cayley-Hamilton then gives a linear recurrence of order $O(|s|)$ for the answer as a function of $n$.

**Derivation.** Generate the first $2D$ answers with the interval DP, where $D$ is the number of states, run Berlekamp-Massey on that prefix, and evaluate the $n$-th term with Kitamasa. The modulus $10007$ is prime, so the divisions in the correction are valid.

**Correctness.** The DP computes the answer for every generated index. The transition depends on the index only through the number of inserted characters, so the answer sequence is generated by a fixed linear map and satisfies its minimal polynomial. Berlekamp-Massey recovers the shortest recurrence of the generated prefix, and the far-term evaluation is the Cayley-Hamilton identity.

**Complexity.** $O(|s|^2 \log n)$ time and $O(|s|)$ memory with the standard $2|s|$-state graph. The first phase generates $O(|s|)$ terms at $O(|s|)$ each, Berlekamp-Massey costs $O(|s|^2)$, and the far-term evaluation costs $O(|s|^2 \log n)$. This is a polynomial improvement over the $O(|s|^3 \log n)$ matrix route.

**Common wrong approach.** Generating too few terms. The recurrence is only guaranteed after about twice its order, and the order is not known in advance; a safe implementation generates several times $|s|$ terms and verifies the recurrence on the extra ones. A second wrong approach is to count insertion sequences instead of distinct palindromes; the DP has to count each final string once.

## Practice progression

1. **Recognition.** Given a sequence and a modulus, decide whether a linear recurrence is plausible, whether the modulus is prime, and whether enough terms are available.
2. **Direct application.** Implement the loop and verify the returned polynomial on the whole prefix. Compare the order with a brute-force solver on sequences of length at most eight over a small prime.
3. **Adaptation.** Compute the next term from the recurrence, then compute the $t$-th term with Kitamasa. Check both against a direct DP for small indices.
4. **Combination.** Take a DP with a fixed state, generate $2D$ terms, recover the recurrence, and compare the far-term answer with matrix exponentiation on a small state space.
5. **Challenge.** Recover a recurrence from a sequence whose generating function is rational, and prove that the order is at most the degree of the denominator.
6. **Transfer.** Use the technique in a counting problem with a large index, in the minimal polynomial of a matrix, or in the linear complexity of a binary stream. The same discrepancy argument appears in all three.
